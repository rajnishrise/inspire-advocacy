Bail

What is the difference between regular bail and anticipatory bail?

Regular bail is sought by a person who has been arrested or is in custody. Anticipatory bail is sought by a person who fears arrest in a non-bailable offence, before any arrest. Under the BNSS, regular bail is dealt with in sections 480 and 483, and anticipatory bail in section 482.

What is ‘default bail’?

If the police do not complete the investigation and file the charge-sheet within the period the law allows (60 or 90 days, depending on the offence; longer for some special statutes), the accused may become entitled to bail as of right. This is often called default or statutory bail (BNSS s. 187(3), formerly CrPC s. 167(2)).

Is bail harder to get in NDPS or excise cases?

Some special laws impose additional conditions. For instance, section 37 of the NDPS Act places stricter limits on bail where a commercial quantity is involved. Each case depends on its facts and the provisions that apply.

What documents are needed for bail?

Usually a copy of the FIR, any court order already passed, identity documents of the accused, and details and documents of the sureties (such as land records or identity proof). Requirements vary by court.

FIR & police

Can an FIR be filed at any police station?

Yes. Information about a cognisable offence can be given at any police station, regardless of where the offence happened. This is called a Zero FIR, and it is later transferred to the police station with jurisdiction (BNSS s. 173).

What if the police refuse to register my FIR?

You can send the information in writing to the Superintendent of Police, and you can approach the Magistrate with an application seeking a direction to register and investigate (BNSS s. 175(3)), or file a complaint case directly (BNSS s. 223).

I received a police notice to appear. What should I do?

A notice under BNSS s. 35(3) (formerly CrPC s. 41A) requires you to appear before the police. Complying with it generally protects against arrest unless the police record reasons. It is sensible to take legal advice before appearing.

Vehicles & property

My vehicle was seized by the police. How can I get it back?

In many cases the owner can apply to the court for interim custody of the vehicle (zimmanama), under BNSS ss. 497 and 503 (formerly CrPC ss. 451 and 457), usually against a bond and an undertaking to produce it when required. Vehicles seized in excise cases may be subject to separate confiscation proceedings.

What documents should I bring for vehicle release?

Registration certificate, insurance, the owner’s identity proof, a copy of the FIR or seizure list, and any finance or hypothecation papers.

New criminal laws

Which law applies to my case, the IPC or the BNS?

Generally, an offence is governed by the law in force on the date it was committed. Offences committed before 1 July 2024 are usually charged under the IPC; those committed on or after that date, under the BNS. Proceedings pending on 1 July 2024 continue under the old procedure (BNSS s. 531).

Why has the section number in my case changed?

The BNS, BNSS and BSA renumbered most provisions. For example, IPC 420 (cheating) corresponds to BNS 318, and CrPC 438 (anticipatory bail) to BNSS 482. See the section table.

What is a vakalatnama?

A vakalatnama is the document by which a client authorises an advocate to appear and act for them in a case. It is filed in court with the first application.
